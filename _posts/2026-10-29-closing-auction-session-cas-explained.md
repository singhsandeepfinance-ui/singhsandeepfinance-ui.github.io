For years, the "closing price" of an Indian stock was not really the price at the close. It was an **average** of trades in the last half-hour. On **3 August 2026**, NSE and BSE switched to a **Closing Auction Session (CAS)** for stocks that trade in futures and options. Then, on **15 October 2026**, SEBI revised the framework after two months of experience. Here is what the CAS is, how it works, and what changes next.

## Why the closing price matters so much

The closing price is not just a number in the newspaper. It is used to:

- settle futures and options contracts
- calculate index values such as the Nifty 50 and Sensex
- value mutual funds, index funds and ETFs (their daily NAV)
- value portfolios, collateral and margins across the market

So if the closing price can be pushed around, the effects spread far beyond one stock.

## The old method and its problem

Until August 2026, the closing price was the **volume-weighted average price (VWAP) of trades between 3:00 pm and 3:30 pm**. SEBI's concern was that large orders placed near the close could distort this average. Passive funds, which must buy or sell at the closing price, especially on index rebalancing days, had no clean way to trade at it. Most large global markets already use an auction at the close.

## How a closing auction works

Instead of averaging trades, the exchange stops normal trading for F&O stocks and opens a short auction:

1. **A reference price is set.** This is the VWAP of the last 15 minutes of normal trading.
2. **Buyers and sellers place orders**, generally within about 3% above or below the reference price. Orders are collected but not matched yet.
3. **The order book closes at a random moment** within the final two minutes. Nobody knows the exact second, so it is hard to slip in a last-instant order to move the price.
4. **The exchange finds one price** at which the largest number of shares can change hands. That becomes the official closing price, and all matched orders trade at it.
5. If no such price can be found, the reference price is used as the close.

Stop-loss and iceberg orders are not allowed in the auction. Market orders are accepted only in its first phase.

## The schedule from 3 August 2026 (current)

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 9:15 am to 3:15 pm | Normal (continuous) trading |
| 3:15 to 3:20 pm | Transition: no new orders; reference price calculated |
| 3:20 to 3:25 pm | Auction order entry: market and limit orders |
| 3:25 to 3:30 pm | Limit orders only; random close somewhere between 3:28 and 3:30 pm |
| 3:30 to 3:35 pm | Orders matched; closing price published |

This applies to **F&O stocks only**, around 200 of them. All other stocks trade normally until 3:30 pm and keep the VWAP-based close. Equity derivatives trading was extended to **3:40 pm**, and many brokers moved their intraday auto square-off earlier.

## What happened in the first two months

The auction proved its value on **index rebalancing days**, exactly the days it was designed for. On **31 August**, when MSCI rebalanced its indices, NSE's closing auction handled turnover of **₹39,718 crore**. On **18 September**, the FTSE rebalancing pushed it to **₹16,613 crore**, the second-highest day since launch. Index funds could buy and sell at the closing price in one place.

The difficulty was with **derivatives settlement**. Expiring futures and options were settled on the auction price, a single price set in a few minutes. Market participants argued this concentrated a very large settlement value on a short window and created a risk of manipulation. SEBI issued a consultation paper on **12 September 2026** to review this.

## Why it is in the news: SEBI's October revision

On **15 October 2026**, SEBI issued a circular revising the CAS. The main changes take effect on **7 December 2026**:

- **The 30-minute VWAP is back for expiry-day settlement.** Index derivatives will settle on the VWAPs of their constituent stocks in the last 30 minutes of trading. Stock derivatives will settle on the stock's VWAP over the same window, across all exchanges. Exchanges must show an indicative value live during that window.
- **The auction moves later and gets shorter.** Normal trading runs to 3:30 pm, and the auction runs from 3:30 to 3:40 pm.
- **Derivatives hours change.** Index and stock derivatives trade until 3:45 pm on normal days. On expiry day, expiring index contracts stop at 3:30 pm.
- **A post-close session** from 3:50 to 4:00 pm lets investors trade at the closing price.

The CAS still decides the **official closing price** of F&O stocks. What changes is that F&O expiry settlement no longer depends on that single auction price.

## The schedule from 7 December 2026

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 9:15 am to 3:30 pm | Normal (continuous) trading |
| 3:30 to 3:31 pm | Transition; reference price = VWAP of the last 15 minutes |
| 3:31 to 3:36 pm | Auction order entry: market and limit orders |
| 3:36 to 3:40 pm | Limit orders only; random close between 3:38 and 3:40 pm |
| After 3:40 pm | Orders matched; closing price published |
| 3:50 to 4:00 pm | Post-close session at the closing price |

A further change, on how unexecuted limit and iceberg orders carry over into the auction, takes effect on **8 February 2027**.

## What it means for you

- **Long-term investors:** very little changes. The closing price you see for large stocks is now set by an auction, which should make it harder to manipulate.
- **If you want the closing price:** you can place an order during the auction window. Your order then trades at the single closing price, if it matches.
- **Check your broker's timings.** Intraday square-off times and the order types allowed near the close have changed and may change again on 7 December.
- **F&O traders:** from 7 December, expiry settlement is based on the last 30 minutes' VWAP again, not on the auction price.

## The takeaway

The closing auction is a move towards global practice: one transparent price at the close, discovered from real supply and demand. Two months of use showed it works well for closing prices and index rebalancing, but that settling derivatives on a few minutes of trading carries its own risks. SEBI's October revision keeps the first and reverses the second. It is a good example of a regulator adjusting a reform after watching how it behaves in practice.
