---
title: "Why NSE's own shares trade on BSE, not on NSE"
description: "India's largest stock exchange listed in September 2026, but on its rival's platform. The reason is a simple rule: a referee cannot also be a player."
topic: markets
---

On **24 September 2026**, the National Stock Exchange of India finally became a listed company. Its shares opened at **₹1,800** against an issue price of **₹1,785**, a modest premium of about 0.8%. But they opened on the **BSE**, not on NSE itself. If you buy NSE shares today, your order goes to its oldest rival. Here is why.

## The IPO in brief

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Listing date | 24 September 2026 |
| Listed on | BSE only |
| Price band | ₹1,700 to ₹1,785 per share |
| Issue price | ₹1,785 (top of the band) |
| Listing price | ₹1,800 (about 0.8% above the issue price) |
| Issue size | About ₹22,560 crore |
| Type | Entirely an **offer for sale**: 12.64 crore shares sold by existing shareholders |
| Subscription | 5.71 times overall: institutions 12.68×, non-institutional investors 6.55×, retail 1.39× |
| Market value at the issue price | About ₹4.42 lakh crore |

Because it was a pure offer for sale, **NSE itself raised no money**. The proceeds went to the shareholders who sold part of their stake. The draft prospectus filed in June 2026 had proposed selling 14.89 crore shares, worth about ₹30,000 crore. The final issue was smaller.

## The rule: an exchange cannot list on itself

The reason is a regulation, not a business choice. **Regulation 45 of SEBI's Securities Contracts (Regulation) (Stock Exchanges and Clearing Corporations) Regulations, 2018**, usually called the SECC Regulations, says that a recognised stock exchange can list its shares only on **another** recognised stock exchange, not on its own platform.

Think of a cricket match where one team also supplies the umpire. Even if the umpire is perfectly honest, the other side, and the crowd, will always wonder. A stock exchange is in exactly that position, because it does four jobs:

1. **It sets and enforces listing rules.** Every listed company must file results, disclose events on time and meet governance norms. The exchange checks this and can penalise or suspend a company. If NSE were listed on NSE, it would be enforcing rules on itself.
2. **It watches trading for manipulation.** Exchanges run surveillance systems that flag unusual price moves and insider trading. Policing trading in its own shares is an obvious conflict.
3. **It sees everyone's orders.** An exchange has a complete view of the order book and trading data. That is a large information advantage over ordinary investors trading its shares.
4. **It is responsible for fair price discovery.** Investors need to trust that a share's price reflects genuine supply and demand. That trust is harder to build when the marketplace and the company are the same entity.

Listing on a rival solves all four problems. BSE, not NSE, is now responsible for checking NSE's disclosures and monitoring trading in its shares.

## This has happened before, in reverse

When **BSE** went public in 2017, it faced the same rule. BSE's shares listed on **NSE** on **3 February 2017**, at a premium of nearly 35%. Nine years later, the favour has been returned: each of India's two big exchanges is now listed on the other's platform.

## Could NSE shares trade on NSE one day?

Possibly, without a formal listing there. Indian rules allow some securities listed on one exchange to be **"permitted to trade"** on another. Around 250 companies already trade on NSE this way while being listed elsewhere. NSE has long argued for this. As early as 2016, it asked SEBI to let its shares trade on NSE as permitted securities, for better liquidity and price discovery.

Ahead of the IPO, NSE's chief executive **Ashishkumar Chauhan** said the exchange could consider seeking this approval later, adding that the listing restriction exists to stop an exchange from regulating itself. As of the most recent reports, no such approval has been announced, and NSE shares trade only on BSE.

## What it means for you as an investor

- **You can still buy NSE shares through any broker.** Your broker simply routes the order to BSE. If you already trade on BSE, nothing changes.
- **All the trading happens on one exchange.** Unlike most large companies, which trade on both NSE and BSE, NSE's shares have a single marketplace for now.
- **There is a small irony.** Every trade in NSE's shares earns transaction fees for its main competitor.
- **The rule protects you.** The separation exists so that investors in an exchange get the same independent oversight as investors in any other listed company.

## The takeaway

NSE listing on BSE is not a snub or a strategy. It is the market's basic principle of independence at work: whoever writes and enforces the rules should not also be judged by them.
