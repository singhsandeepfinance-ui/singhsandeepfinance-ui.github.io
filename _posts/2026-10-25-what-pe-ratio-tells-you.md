Open any stock page and the price-to-earnings ratio is one of the first numbers you see. It is useful, but only if you know what it measures.

## The calculation

The P/E ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share (EPS):

> P/E = Share price ÷ Earnings per share

If a share trades at ₹500 and the company earned ₹25 per share over the last year, the P/E is 20. Put another way, buyers are paying ₹20 for every ₹1 of yearly profit. Flip it over and you get the **earnings yield**: 25 ÷ 500 = 5%.

## What a high or low P/E suggests

A **high P/E** usually means the market expects profits to grow quickly, or sees the business as unusually safe. A **low P/E** can mean the stock is cheap, or that the market expects profits to fall. The number alone cannot tell you which.

## Four checks before you trust it

**1. Which earnings?** A *trailing* P/E uses the last twelve months of actual profit. A *forward* P/E uses analysts' estimates for the next year. They can differ a lot, so make sure you compare like with like.

**2. Compare within an industry.** Banks, software companies and cement makers have very different typical P/E levels. A P/E of 30 may be cheap for one sector and expensive for another.

**3. Watch out for one-off items.** A large one-time gain, such as selling a property, inflates earnings and makes the P/E look low. A one-time loss does the opposite.

**4. Be careful with cyclical businesses.** For companies in metals, autos or commodities, profits swing with the economic cycle. Their P/E often looks lowest at the peak of the cycle, just before profits fall.

## A step further: growth

The PEG ratio divides the P/E by the expected yearly earnings growth rate (in %). A company with a P/E of 30 growing earnings at 30% a year has a PEG of 1, which some investors see as fair value. It is a rough guide that depends heavily on the growth forecast being right.

## The takeaway

The P/E ratio is a starting point for questions, not an answer. Use it to compare similar companies, check what earnings it is based on, and always ask *why* the market is paying the price it is.
